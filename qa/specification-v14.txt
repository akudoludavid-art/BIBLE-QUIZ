6. AUTHORING STANDARD — A QUESTION MUST BE WELL ASKED
================================================================================

Apply ALL rules to every existing and new item. Use the user's explicit “well asked” requirement as a release gate, not an aspiration.

1. Write natural, literate, concise English. Use a complete question with correct syntax, spelling, punctuation and natural verb choice.
2. Identify the intended person, dynasty, period, place or source context when ambiguity might otherwise exist.
3. Provide enough distinction to make the question FAIR without inserting the answer into the stem.
4. Specify whether the question is “according to 2 Kings”, “in Matthew's account”, “as described in Acts”, etc. whenever multiple accounts differ.
5. In multiple-choice, exactly ONE distinct option must satisfy EVERY stated condition. If there are multiple correct answers, rewrite the question or use an explicitly multi-select question type only if already intentionally supported and tested. Preserve standard four-choice MC by default.
6. Correct option and distractors should be the same semantic type and a similar level of description.
7. No “all of the above,” “none of the above,” absurd nonsense, or obviously wrong era/category fillers unless genuinely required by a specifically verified question.
8. Never expose the answer through unusual capitalization, length, grammatical agreement, singular/plural mismatch, specificity, reference formatting, or “one answer is much longer” cues.
9. For statements by biblical characters, distinguish who SPOKE them from who RECORDED them, and who they addressed.
10. Use careful causality: “was killed by,” “ordered the killing of,” “was implicated in,” “claimed to have killed,” “died after being wounded by,” “was executed by” do not mean the same thing.
11. Do not use “never”, “only”, “first”, “last”, “every”, “none”, or “the first to” without full canonical verification.
12. When questions require exact quotations, verify the exact translation and preserve orthography; otherwise paraphrase accurately, avoiding misquotation marks.
13. “Where did it happen?” is not interchangeable with “where was this person buried?”
14. A king's origin or kingdom is not necessarily identical to the city where he died, was wounded, fled, or was buried.
15. A narrative's sequence is not always a precise historical chronology across multiple books.
16. For ancient places, use the period-appropriate place name and where relevant distinguish identical names or uncertain modern sites.
17. Avoid requiring invented names for people whom the Bible leaves unnamed. “The unnamed archer” is not a named historical individual.
18. Do not misrepresent inference, tradition or later folklore as an explicit biblical statement.
19. Avoid irrelevant theological polemics. Test what the text records, not unqualified denominational interpretation.
20. If three same-type plausible distractors cannot be found, revise the question, its degree of specificity, or the distractor pool rather than insert poor options.

ILLUSTRATIVE BAD→GOOD REWRITES (VERIFY IN THE TARGET EDITION):
BAD: “Who killed Jehoram?”  GOOD: “Who shot Joram (Jehoram), the son of Ahab and king of Israel, when Jehu met him near Jezreel?” (2 Kings 9:21–24).
BAD: “Who killed Ahab?”  GOOD: “Which king of Israel was wounded in battle by an unnamed archer despite disguising himself at Ramoth-gilead?” (1 Kings 22:29–35). Never claim Scripture names the archer.
BAD: “How did Saul die?” with incompatible options and unqualified alternate claims. GOOD: “According to the narrative in 1 Samuel 31:3–5, what did Saul do after being wounded by archers?” Explain separately the Amalekite's testimony in 2 Samuel 1:6–10.
BAD: “What was Bethel called?” GOOD: “What earlier name does Genesis 28:19 give for the place Jacob named Bethel?” Correct: Luz.
BAD: “What does Luke say about X?” with no identifiable passage. GOOD: cite scene/context without giving answer away.
BAD: “Who is described by this story? [a long biography copied verbatim from the character study list]” GOOD: an original, precisely sourced clue with a focused discriminating detail, avoiding answer leakage and generic story templates.
BAD: “Which children or descendants are specifically associated with this character in this quiz?” with giant comma-separated answer lists. GOOD: a precise relationship question such as which named son succeeded the father; or an exact documented mother-child connection.

QUESTIONS WITH NEGATION: “Which of these was NOT...” only when meaningful and verified. Mark NOT visibly/accessibly; prefer positive questions unless the negative contrast is the actual skill being tested. Never make the right answer conspicuously unlike all other options.

7. INTELLIGENT DISTRACTOR SELECTION — A MAJOR HARD REQUIREMENT
================================================================================

The current generic/random near() and distractors() approach is insufficient for the proposed difficult bank. Introduce a structured candidate scorer with entity type, verified properties, and difficulty-aware similarity. For each authored item, permit a hand-curated, independently checked set of options; for generated items, derive candidates from graph constraints.

CHARACTER DISTRACTOR RULES:
- If the answer is a clearly male biblical character, prioritize three male character distractors. If clearly female, prioritize female distractors. Do not infer sex from ambiguous names; where not established, seek other meaningful similarity rather than force gender.
- Match relevant role: king with king, priest with priest, prophet with prophet, military officer with officer, ruler with ruler, mother with mother, servant with servant, apostle with apostle.
- Match related timeline: same reign, overlapping period, neighboring generation, immediate predecessor/successor, related dynasty.
- Match narrative relationship: same battle, household, chapter, political crisis, traveling party, royal court, prophetic mission, civil conflict, or geographic territory.
- Match ancestry or dynasty where useful: relatives, spouses, siblings, in-laws, heirs; but do NOT accidentally offer an alternate name for the same person as a separate wrong answer.
- Match name pitfalls: legitimate near-identical names of distinct people (e.g. multiple Herods, Ananias, Joram/Jehoram, James, Judas, Zechariah) with enough qualifiers to be unambiguous when displayed.

PLACE DISTRACTOR RULES:
- Cities against cities, mountains against mountains, regions against regions, alt-place names against genuine alt-place names.
- Prefer places in the same travel corridor, tribal region, neighboring territory or historical setting, not random distant places.
- Never use two names of the SAME location as one correct and one incorrect unless the stem specifically asks for exact historical wording/period and the distinction is defensible.

OTHER TYPES:
- Battle questions: comparable battles, contemporaneous political entities, nearby sites, plausible commanders.
- Sacrifice questions: distinct genuine offerings or legal procedures with the same legal vocabulary, not obviously unrelated narratives.
- King questions: same kingdom / nearby reign / similar events, with correctly disambiguated Israel vs Judah.
- Chronology questions: alternative sequences using the SAME actual entities, with plausible reversals; independently validate the true order.
- Cross-Testament questions: prophets or books plausibly quoted nearby, not three arbitrary people.
- Relationship questions: comparable relationship distances (uncle/grandfather/father-in-law etc.) with all alternatives well-formed.
- Ruler-death questions: alternatives with similar manner/circumstances, but one exact person, place, action or scripture distinction makes the difference.

CANDIDATE SCORING (GUIDELINE, NOT A LICENSE TO INVENT):
+ high similarity for identical type/office
+ high similarity for direct same-era / same-reign links
+ high similarity for shared place/kingdom / same narrative event
+ moderate similarity for same dynasty / family / first-name overlap
+ moderate similarity for comparable known cause-of-death or source book
- reject if identical entity under an alias
- reject if candidate is also correct under the literal wording
- reject if candidate relies on uncertain/unsupported attributes
- reject if visibly implausible, spoiler-like, duplicate, or wrong grammatical type
- reject if all distractors differ wildly in length or detail from the answer

Enforce MINIMUM QUALITY: each of the three distractors should have at least one genuine relatedness link; Advanced/Mastery should aim for 2–3 meaningful links per distractor whenever the database permits it. A scoring algorithm is not a substitute for proof: verify the question and all four candidates separately.

DO NOT use runtime AI to dynamically invent distractors. Ship a reviewed static/derived bank and deterministic, fact-anchored template generator. For generated questions, retain a source record and sufficient proof metadata. Support curated override when necessary.

Implement a semantic correctness check: a candidate's alias, equivalence, role, same-name ambiguity or another valid event must not make the supposedly wrong answer defensible. If no valid set of exactly three wrong answers exists, exclude that item and report why.

11. BIBLICAL EDGE-CASE TEST FIXTURES — CHECK THE ENGINE AGAINST THESE
================================================================================

Use the following as editorial QA CASES, not as an instruction to blindly copy every formulation. Verify the passages in the edition selected.

01. Joram (Jehoram) son of Ahab, Israel, was shot by Jehu; Joram/Jeho(r)am king of Judah is a different person. (2 Kings 9; 2 Kings 8.)
02. Jehu personally shot Joram; he ordered action against Ahaziah and Jezebel; distinguish his role from the agents who physically carried out every killing. (2 Kings 9:21–37; 2 Chronicles 22:7–9.)
03. Ahab was wounded by an unnamed archer in battle. Do not invent a named shooter. (1 Kings 22:29–35.)
04. Saul fell on his sword in the narrator's account; an Amalekite later claimed he had killed him. Preserve narrator/claim distinction. (1 Samuel 31:3–5; 2 Samuel 1:6–10.)
05. King Amon's servants killed him, conspirators were then killed by people of the land, and his son Josiah was made king. (2 Kings 21:23–26.)
06. Ebed-melech acted to rescue Jeremiah from a cistern; distinguish hostile officials in the same passage. (Jeremiah 38:1–13.)
07. The old name Luz is associated with Bethel, while Laish/Dan and Jebus/Jerusalem are separate naming examples with their own textual nuances. (Genesis 28:19; Judges 18:29; Judges 19:10.)
08. The son of Jehoiada named Zechariah was killed at Joash's command; distinguish other Zechariahs. (2 Chronicles 24:20–22.)
09. Different Herods appear in Jesus' early life, John the Baptist's death, Acts 12 and Paul's hearings. Exact titles and identity matter. (Matthew 2; Mark 6; Acts 12; Acts 25–26.)
10. Amos confronted by Amaziah at Bethel, in the context of Jeroboam II; distinguish Amos from contemporaries. (Amos 7:10–17.)
11. Jehoiada the priest and Joash: direction of rescue and later death must not be confused with Zechariah's death. (2 Kings 11; 2 Chronicles 22–24.)
12. Stephen quotes Amos in Acts, not just whichever prophet wrote similar warnings. (Acts 7:42–43; Amos 5:25–27.)
13. Matthew applies Hosea's words about God's son and Egypt to Jesus. Differentiate original OT context and NT application. (Matthew 2:15; Hosea 11:1.)
14. Rahab → Boaz → Obed → Jesse → David as presented in Matthew 1 and Ruth 4: distinguish grandfather/great-grandfather from further ancestors, and be careful about genealogical telescoping. (Matthew 1:5–6; Ruth 4:17–22.)
15. The same NAME in the same historical cluster must not collapse two kings into one. Check both Ahaziah and Jehoram repetitions. (1–2 Kings; 2 Chronicles.)
16. Book of the Law found under Josiah, Huldah consulted, Hilkiah high priest, Shaphan the scribe; questions should separate discoverer, reader, messenger, prophetess and king. (2 Kings 22.)
17. Abner, Joab, Asahel, Ish-bosheth, Baanah and Rechab have interconnected but different roles in the succession/conflict narrative. (2 Samuel 2–4.)
18. Peter's imprisonment under Agrippa I and James son of Zebedee's execution; don't mistake different James figures. (Acts 12.)
19. Antioch in Pisidia vs Antioch in Syria: don't conflate Paul and Barnabas's route with their sending community. (Acts 13–14.)
20. Elisha at Dothan vs events at Carmel under Elijah; cross-book parallel character traps must be explicitly checked. (1 Kings 18; 2 Kings 6.)
21. Elijah, Elisha, Gehazi, Hazael, Naaman and the Jordan; a character may command a healing ritual rather than physically enact it. (2 Kings 5–8.)
22. Athaliah's rule in Judah disrupts simplistic male-king-only succession chains. (2 Kings 11; 2 Chronicles 22–23.)
23. Chronology of Jerusalem's fall, Jehoiachin's captivity and Zedekiah's reign must follow explicitly cited accounts, not generalized “the exile.” (2 Kings 24–25; Jeremiah 39 and 52.)
24. KJV verses can contain different wording, names or punctuation from WEB. Never present WEB wording as an exact KJV quotation or vice versa.
25. Do not infer a person's death method from tradition where Scripture leaves it unstated; withheld question preferred over a fabricated answer.
26. Ambiguous maps: a plausible place modern coordinate or an ancient region name is not necessarily an established archaeological location. If uncertain, formulate question on textual location only or mark it as debated and exclude from objective single-choice.
27. “Who was killed by Saul?” is not the same as “Whose killing did Saul order?” or “Who died in a battle commanded by Saul?” Check causative agency.
28. “Which prophet could not overlap?” requires rigorous historical evidence; unsupported assumptions based only on when a book was written are insufficient.
29. Similar name of father does NOT establish family relation; use ID-specific evidence.
30. For a negative question, prove all other alternatives are true under the exact stated conditions; verify the one negative with explicit evidence.

Create at least one automated test / editorial fixture for each hazard class above. Add further edge cases discovered during the source audit.
